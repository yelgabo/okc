1. Where the time goes, shape by shape

Twelve scored shapes, each with our bar above the public entry's. Bar length is the judged time; the segments are each profile's kernel rows grouped by component and scaled to that total, because under programmatic dependent launch the rows overlap and add up to more than the step. Sliding-window shapes count 46 times per step, full-context shapes 16 times, so the top of each pair matters most.

2. One decode token, sliding layer: the launch chain

Each box is one kernel launch, drawn to its profile time; a thin gap is a dependent launch the GPU must wait for; a chevron into a box means that kernel was launched early by programmatic dependent launch (PDL) and only waits for the data it reads. Our router chain runs on a second stream under the pooler. Theirs runs one stream and chains every kernel.

3. What is built differently inside each piece

Three mechanisms account for most of the kernel-level gap. In each panel the left half is ours, the right half the public entry; the colored element is the part that changes.

Attention: how splits are merged

weight-46 shapes: 14.5 µs vs 4.9 µs at decode 1

Ours exchanges a running max through partial buffers and merges in a second launch (or a cluster barrier on sliding layers). Theirs never exchanges a max: QK-norm bounds every logit, so 2^score stays in FP32 range and every split adds straight into one workspace with atomics. No combine launch, no memset, and at batch 1 a plan of 32 CTAs with one 64-key tile each.

MoE: how the expert GEMMs are scheduled

verify 32 sliding: 381 µs vs 198 µs of MoE rows

At 128 and 256 tokens our kernel is bound by traffic between L2 and the SMs: each weight byte crosses 1.5 times because a hot expert is split into 8 to 16-token items, and mma.sync cannot take larger tiles without saturating the shared-memory port. Register-A wgmma, with the dequantized weights in registers and tokens read through a descriptor, takes 64-token jobs. At one to eight tokens the public kernel saves the three launch hops and the fill and drain of four grids.

Dense projections: who reduces, and how many bytes

decode 16: 74 µs vs 51 µs of dense rows

Our single-token GEMVs already sit on the judge's streaming line (1.3 µs plus bytes at 4.05 TB/s), so the public entry's smaller numbers come from moving 25% fewer bytes, not from a better load path. Above eight tokens the difference is one launch instead of two and no reduction across CTAs. Above 32 tokens both entries keep cuBLAS.
